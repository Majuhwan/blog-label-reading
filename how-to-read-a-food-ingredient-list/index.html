How to Read a Food Ingredient List, Order, Names, and Fine Print

What federal rules say about ingredient lists: why order matters, the 2 percent rule, how blends and sub-ingredients appear, and what natural flavor means.

Label ReadingOct 11, 2026 · 5 min read

The Nutrition Facts panel tells you how much of each nutrient a serving contains. The ingredient list tells you what the food is actually made of, and it follows strict federal rules about order and wording. Once you know those rules, a short list of words tells you a lot. This guide is based on two FDA regulations: 21 CFR 101.4, which covers how ingredients are named and ordered, and 21 CFR 101.22, which covers spices and flavors.

Rule 1: The order is by weight

The core rule is in 101.4(a)(1): ingredients must be listed by their common or usual name in descending order of predominance by weight. The first ingredient is the one present in the greatest amount by weight, the second is the next largest, and so on.

That makes the first few ingredients the most informative part of the list. If sugar appears first in a cereal, there is more sugar by weight than any other single ingredient.

Two cautions follow from "by weight":

  • Weight is not volume. A light, bulky ingredient can take up a lot of space and still appear lower on the list than a dense one.
  • Order is not percentage. The list tells you which ingredient is heavier, not by how much, unless the label adds percentages.

Rule 2: The "2 percent or less" exception

There is one exception to strict ordering. Under 101.4(a)(2), ingredients present at 2 percent or less by weight can be grouped at the end of the list, out of order, after a phrase such as "Contains 2 percent or less of" or "Less than 2 percent of."

The threshold in that phrase can be 2 percent, or, if the manufacturer prefers, 1.5, 1.0, or 0.5 percent. The rule is strict about one thing: no ingredient in that group may exceed the stated threshold.

So everything after "contains 2% or less of" is a minor ingredient by weight, but the ingredients within that group can appear in any order.

Rule 3: Compound ingredients can be broken down two ways

Many foods contain ingredients that are themselves made of several ingredients, such as chocolate chips in a cookie. Under 101.4(b)(2), the label can do either of the following:

  • Name the ingredient and list its contents in parentheses, in descending order, for example "chocolate chips (sugar, chocolate, cocoa butter)."
  • Fold every component into the main list, each in its overall order of predominance in the finished food, without naming the compound ingredient at all.

With the second option, you will not see the compound ingredient's name at all; its components are spread through the main list.

Rule 4: Fats, oils, and "and/or"

Fats and oils get detailed rules in 101.4(b)(14). Each fat or oil must be declared by its specific name, such as "cottonseed oil" or "beef fat." Blends can be listed as, for example, "vegetable oil shortening (soybean and cottonseed oil)."

The rule also explains a phrase that confuses many shoppers. A manufacturer may list fats or oils that are not always present, as long as words such as "or," "and/or," or "contains one or more of the following" make that clear. The regulation's example: "vegetable oil shortening (contains one or more of the following: cottonseed oil, palm oil, soybean oil)." That flexibility does not apply when fats and oils are the main ingredient of the product; then only oils actually present may be listed.

The same rule says hydrogenated oils must be named "hydrogenated" or "partially hydrogenated" as applicable.

Rule 5: Some names are allowed to be general

Specific names are the default, but 101.4(b) lists permitted shortcuts. A few that show up often:

On the label Can cover
Milk Milk, concentrated milk, reconstituted milk, dry whole milk
Nonfat milk or skim milk Skim milk, concentrated or reconstituted skim milk, nonfat dry milk
Whey Whey, concentrated, reconstituted, or dried whey
Eggs Dried, frozen, or liquid whole eggs
Sugar Sucrose from sugar cane or sugar beets

The last row is useful: on an ingredient list, "sugar" means sucrose specifically. Other sweeteners appear under their own names.

Spices and flavors

Here 101.22 sets the rules.

Spice. Defined as an aromatic plant substance used mainly for seasoning, such as cinnamon, cumin, oregano, or black pepper. Onions, garlic, and celery are not counted as spices, because they are traditionally regarded as foods. Spices may be declared simply as "spice." Paprika, turmeric, and saffron, which also add color, must be declared as "spice and coloring" unless named individually.

Natural flavor. Defined as an essential oil, extract, distillate, or similar product whose flavoring comes from a natural source, including spices, fruit, vegetables, herbs, meat, seafood, poultry, eggs, dairy, or fermentation products, and whose main function is flavor rather than nutrition. It may be declared simply as "natural flavor."

Artificial flavor. Any flavoring substance not derived from those natural sources. It may be declared as "artificial flavor."

Powders count as foods. Garlic powder, onion powder, and celery powder are considered food, not flavor, and must be listed by name.

A worked example

Here is a hypothetical granola list, read with these rules:

Ingredients: Whole grain oats, sugar, vegetable oil (contains one or more of the following: canola oil, sunflower oil), honey, contains 2% or less of salt, cinnamon, natural flavor.

  • Oats are the heaviest ingredient, and sugar is the second heaviest.
  • The oil blend may not contain both oils in every batch.
  • Salt, cinnamon, and natural flavor each make up 2 percent or less by weight, in no required order.

Key takeaways

  • Ingredients are listed in descending order by weight, so the first few matter most.
  • Items after "contains 2% or less of" are each at or below that threshold and need not be in order.
  • Compound ingredients can be shown in parentheses or blended into the main list.
  • "And/or" in an oil listing means not every oil named is always present.
  • On a label, "sugar" means sucrose, and "natural flavor" has a specific federal definition.
